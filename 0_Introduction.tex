\noindent
Cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide with around 20 million cases reported in 2022. This corresponds to one in five men or women developing cancer in their lifetime \cite{bray_global_2024}. The conventional method for cancer diagnosis, as outlined in Figure \ref{fig:hist-summary}, involves taking a biopsy of suspect tissue from a patient during surgery, fixing this biopsy to a slide, and then staining the biopsy with haematoxylin and eosin (H\&E). The haematoxylin stains nucleic acids a deep blue-purple colour, whilst the eosin is pink and non-specifically stains proteins \cite{fischer_hematoxylin_2008}. These H\&E stained samples can then be analysed by a pathologist to develop a diagnosis. This process is manual, error-prone, subjective, costly, and time-consuming; often requiring transport to a laboratory for processing by highly trained technicians \cite{hollon_near_2020}. The sample processing time alone is often 20 minutes or more \cite{novis_interinstitutional_1997}, so the development of methods which forgo traditional lab-based preparation have significant potential to guide surgeries more effectively.

New methods are being developed to expedite the cancer diagnosis process by using machine learning in conjunction with a range of imaging technologies including stimulated Raman spectroscopy (SRS) \cite{hollon_near_2020, sarri_fast_2019, kondepudi_foundation_2024, jiang_opensrh_2022}, second harmonic generation microscopy (SHG) \cite{sarri_fast_2019}, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) \cite{tomas_detection_2022, berisha_deep_2019}. Within this work, I focus on the application of FTIR spectroscopy to cancer diagnosis within colon cancer samples provided by collaborators at the University of Exeter, which were collected and had FTIR spectral images produced as part of the MINERVA project \cite{Minerva}.
\subsection{Future Vision}
The TROPHY project \cite{Trophy} is aiming to develop a new vibrational spectroscopic imaging microscope exploiting photo-thermal infrared (PTIR), FTIR and digital holography microscopy (DMH) to evaluate cell-type topologies, identify mutant cell clusters, and provide biochemical analyses to determine tumour types, grading and staging \cite{Trophy_proposal}. The MINERVA and TROPHY datasets are expected to be similar, so the models and methods developed in this work constitute a basis for future work towards the TROPHY goals. This is particularly apparent given that TROPHY will focus primarily on the same fingerprint $1000$-$1800$ cm$^{-1}$ region of the IR spectrum as MINERVA.

Other works which could benefit from the approaches described here include work on the CHARM project which is also taking place in Cambridge. This project is similar to TORPHY in that they are devleoping a new microscope for cancer diagnosis. CHARM are instead focussing on Raman, SGH and TPEF signals, as these have been shown to have some advantages over FTIR \cite{things}. Currently, data produced by that microscope is very noisy, and also only has intensity information at around 20 wavelengths, limiting the viability of the SCNN method implemented in this work. However, as the microscopy performance improves, it should be possible to apply the same methods as described here. Some work was done throughout this project to remove vignetting effects in the CHARM dataset. However, this was only pursued briefly, so information relating to it is attached in appendix \ref{sec:charm}

\subsection{Novelty of This Work}
This work is focusses on developing a deep learning method for cancer identification from FTIR data which is both explainable and generalisable. This is achieved by first implementing a physically-motivated convolutional neural network, before then utilising a context-representative method to create a latent space from within which different clusters can be identified to elucidate the multiple pathways by which an individual classification such as cancer or non-cancer can be achieved. No other work has been found to use a similar method, although others have used the context-representative method to extract different spectral contributions within urine samples \cite{crine}. This work then further extends this method, by utilising the CRIME predictions of average spectra spectra and weightings describing the importance of each wavelength within those spectra, to then produce a simple weighted cosine similarity model for classification into non-cancer, hyperplastic, adenoma and cancerous classes.
