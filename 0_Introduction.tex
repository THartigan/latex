Cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide with around 20 million cases reported in 2022. This corresponds to one in five men or women developing cancer in their lifetime \cite{bray_global_2024}. The conventional method for cancer diagnosis, as outlined in Figure \ref{fig:hist-summary}, involves taking a biopsy of suspect tissue from a patient during surgery, fixing this biopsy to a slide, and then staining the biopsy with haematoxylin and eosin (H\&E). The haematoxylin stains nucleic acids a deep blue-purple colour, whilst the eosin is pink and non-specifically stains proteins \cite{fischer_hematoxylin_2008}. These H\&E stained samples can then be analysed by a pathologist to develop a diagnosis. This process is manual, error-prone, subjective, costly, and time-consuming; often requiring transport to a laboratory for processing by highly trained technicians \cite{hollon_near_2020}. The sample processing time alone is often 20 minutes or more \cite{novis_interinstitutional_1997}, so the development of methods which forgo traditional lab-based preparation have significant potential to guide surgeries more effectively.

New methods are being developed to exploit machine learning (ML) in conjunction with a range of imaging technologies to expedite cancer diagnosis. The imaging technologies under investigation include stimulated Raman spectroscopy (SRS) \cite{hollon_near_2020, sarri_fast_2019, kondepudi_foundation_2024, jiang_opensrh_2022}, second harmonic generation microscopy (SHG) \cite{sarri_fast_2019}, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) \cite{tomas_detection_2022, berisha_deep_2019,tang_advancing_2024,sala_liquid_2022,duckworth_frontline_2023}. Within this work, I focus on the application of FTIR spectroscopy to cancer identification and classification within colon cancer samples provided by collaborators at the University of Exeter \cite{nallala_high-resolution_2016,noauthor_minerva_nodate}.

\subsection{Previous Works}
% maybe move the Tomas reference to where I am describing the MINERVA data class differences in Background
Previous works using vibrational spectroscopy methods have reported high classification performance for cancer identification. For example, Lasalvia et al. \cite{lasalvia_discrimination_2023} report cancer vs non-cancer classification accuracies from $87\%$ to almost $100\%$ for colorectal cancer samples using ML techniques on FTIR spectra. Furthermore, work by Tiwari et al. \cite{tiwari_colon_2022} has achieved $97\%$ and $80\%$ colon tumour classification accuracy for high/moderate and low-grade tumours respectively using a pre-trained ResNet and convolutional network approach. Tomas et al. \cite{tomas_detection_2022} have used attenuated total reflection FTIR (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy and feed-forward full-connected neural networks to classify breast cancer samples into benign and malignant classifications with $96.06\% \pm 7.07 \%$ accuracy. They found the different tissue types within breast cancer exhibit large differences in spectral characteristics as can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:breast-tomas-benign-malignant-diff}. Some works \cite{he_accurate_2022} have also explored the use of variational autoencoders (VAEs) in classification tasks. However, their applicability has been limited to de-noising input Raman spectral data. These advances demonstrate the potential for vibrational spectroscopy and machine learning in the field of cancer detection, which I aim to build on in this work.

\subsection{Novelty of this Work}
In this work, I present three main contributions. Firstly, weakly-labelled data is used to train a scale-invariant convolutional neural network (SCNN) for the classification of colon cancer samples into four classes: non-cancer, hyperplastic, adenoma and cancerous. Secondly, I apply explainability methods on this model to elucidate the multiple pathways by which these classifications can be achieved. Finally, these explainability results are used to create a simple weighted cosine similarity model for classification into the same four classes. The SCNN and explainability methods are inspired by contributions by Zaki et al. \cite{zaki_explainable_2024}, who used a similar approach to identify  serotonin-dominated VAE latent space regions from the FTIR spectra of urine samples.
