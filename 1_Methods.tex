\section{Theory and Methods}
\subsection{FTIR Spectroscopy}
%Check which of the CHARM methods are actually needed here and potentially don't bother with them all

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy works by directing a broadband spectrum with wavenumbers ranging from around $400-4000 \text{ cm}^{-1}$ (for mid-IR spectroscopy) \cite{su_fourier_2020} onto a sample. This coherent spectrum then excites resonant vibrational modes within molecular bonds inside the sample, causing absorption of specific frequencies depending on the composition of the incident sample. This decreases the intensity at the absorbed frequencies relative to the broadband baseline, generating a characteristic spectrum for each vibrational mode. The frequencies at which these vibrations occur are ultimately determined by quantum electron density effects \cite{}. However, they are often well modelled by a simple harmonic oscillator approach \cite{} subject to the requirement that the molecular dipole moment $\mu$ can change \cite{}. For the bond demonstrated in Figure \ref{fig:ir_bond}, we therefore expect IR absorption at frequency:
\begin{equation}
    \nu = \frac{1}{2\pi c} \sqrt{\frac{k(m_1+m_2)}{m_1 m_2}}.
\end{equation}
The requirement for molecules to be polar results in FTIR being particularly sensitive to functional groups with high electronegativity differences like those depicted in Figure \ref{fig:ir_bond}. \\

\noindent
Modern devices use a Fourier transform-based (FTIR) interferogram approach \cite{griffiths_fourier_1983}, enabling high signal-to-noise (SNR) ratios to be achieved. FTIR can detect biochemical features including nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates within biological samples by matching the observed spectra with known forms for different bonding types, functional groups and intermolecular interactions \cite{su_fourier_2020}. Typical absorption groups within biological samples are detailed in Table \ref{table:functional_groups}. Furthermore, we expect FTIR to be a good for cancer detection, as cancerous growth is often associated with changes in the acidity of tissues, and changes in acidity are known to cause hydrogen-bonding induced changes to FTIR spectral peak positions \cite{wolpert_infrared_2006,torii_modeling_2024,czowski_cancer_2020}. This effect is demonstrated in Figure \ref{fig:ir_differences_ph}.

\subsection{Cross-Validation Approach}
\label{sec:methods:data_split}
FTIR data was produced for 90 samples, of which 31 were classified as normal, 17 as hyperplastic, 27 as adenomatous, and 15 as cancerous. Due to the desire to accurately predict performance in four-category prediction tasks, a 4-fold cross-validation approach was adopted \cite{kohavi_study_2001}. This involved splitting the dataset into four parts using the stratified group K-fold method, which produces splits subject to the constraints of both preserving the proportion of samples (pixels) for each class within each split to the maximum extent possible whilst also enforcing the requirement for non-overlapping groups (images) between splits \cite{t_r_stratified_2023,gunopulos_stratification_2011, szymanski_network_2017}. When training ML models on spectral data, an approach was adopted where for each set of four folds, two were used for training, one for validation and one for testing. For methods which did not require validation, the validation set was discarded, enabling maximal comparability between methods.

\subsection{Baseline Classification Methods}
\label{sec:methods:baseline_classification}
The most intuitive approach for classifying spectra according to their severity is to calculate the mean spectrum for each classification within the training set and then to measure which mean spectrum most closely resembles a test spectrum. Similarly to work by Villazon et al. \cite{villazon_cancer_2025}, this work implements a mean square error (MSE) method, and a cosine similarity method. The mean square error is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MSE}(\vec x,\vec y) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2
\end{equation}
where $\vec x$ and $\vec y$ are the spectra being compared, and $n$ is the number of wavenumbers (dimensions) in each spectrum. The cosine similarity is also defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Cosine Similarity}(\vec x, \vec y) = \frac{\vec x \cdot \vec y}{|\vec x| |\vec y|},
\end{equation}
which produces values between $-1$ and $1$ with $-1$ denoting complete orthogonality and $1$ denoting complete similarity between the two spectra. The classification of each test spectrum is then set as that which corresponds to the mean spectrum which minimises the MSE or maximises the cosine similarity.

\subsection{Machine Learning (ML) For Classification}
\subsubsection{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}
\label{sec:methods:pca}
PCA \cite{wold_principal_nodate} is a dimensionality reduction technique which aims to project high-dimensional data into a new, more meaningful basis by re-expressing the data through a linear combination of these new basis vectors. This linearity allows us to pose the problem through linear algebra, where we wish to find a linear transformation $\boldsymbol{P}$ which maps the original recorded dataset $\bf X$ to a new representation of that dataset $\boldsymbol{Y}$ such that:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{Y} = \boldsymbol{P} \cdot \boldsymbol{X}.
\end{equation}
The rows of $\boldsymbol{P}$ then form a new set of orthonormal basis vectors. To find $\bf P$, we assume that axes of interest are those with high variance. These high variance axes can be found by calculating the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the dataset: 
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{C} = \frac{1}{n} \boldsymbol{X} \boldsymbol{X}^T,
\end{equation}
which itself gives the degree of linear relationship between different variables. The eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{C}$ therefore correspond to the variance of $\boldsymbol{X}$ along the associated eigenvector \cite{shlens_tutorial_2014}. Throughout this work, PCA is used to reduce inputs to 25 dimensions.

\subsubsection{Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)}
\label{sec:methods:lda}
LDA \cite{fisher_use_1936} is a classification technique which aims to maximise the separability of classifications for given input data (e.g. IR spectra) by maximising between-class variance and minimising within-class variance \cite{tharwat_linear_2017}. This is achieved by assuming data corresponding to a specific class $k$ is normally distributed about a mean value $\mu_k$, and that all classes have equal covariance matrices $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$. The first assumption requires there to be a point $x^*$ which is equally likely to belong to two classes. If we take $x^*$ as the decision boundary, we can then minimise the classification error, which in the binary classification case with classes $\mathcal C_1$ and $\mathcal C_2$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
   \mathbb{P}(\text{error})= \mathbb{P}(x>x^*, x\in \mathcal C_1) + \mathbb{P}(x<x^*, x\in \mathcal C_2).
\end{equation}
Minimising this error with respect to $x^*$, denoting the probability density within each class as $f_k(x)$ and denoting priors for each class by $\pi_k$ then gives;
\begin{equation}
    f_1(x^*)\pi_1 = f_2(x^*)\pi_2.
\end{equation}
Now considering multi-class classification and assuming the data follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution of dimension $d$, we have;
\begin{equation}
    f_k(\boldsymbol{x})\pi_k=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_k|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)\right)\pi_k.
\end{equation}
Taking the logarithm of this, discounting constant terms and enforcing the equal covariance assumption gives scaled posterior probabilities for each class $k$ as:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_k(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x} - \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_k + \ln(\pi_k).
\end{equation}
The classification $k$ with maximal $\delta_k(\boldsymbol{x})$ is then chosen as the predicted class for $\boldsymbol{x}$ \cite{ghojogh_linear_2019}. In practice, the priors $\pi_k$ are estimated according to the proportion of samples in each class, and the means $\boldsymbol \mu_k$ and covariance matrices $\boldsymbol{\Sigma_k}$ are estimated using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE \cite{haynes_maximum_2013}. In the mean case, this is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_i \mathbb{I}(\mathcal C(\boldsymbol x_i)=k),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}$ is the indicator function \cite{ghojogh_linear_2019}. As we assume the covariance matrices are the same for each class, we take $\boldsymbol \Sigma$ to be the weighted sum of the estimated covariance matrices for each class. This can be considered geometrically \cite{mika_fisher_1999,tharwat_linear_2017} by solving an eigenvalue problem as is shown in Figure \ref{fig:LDA}. New data can then be classified by finding the projected training class mean which minimises the distance to the projection of test data within the eigenvector space.

\subsubsection{Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost or XGB)}
\label{sec:methods:xgboost}
XGBoost generates an ensemble of regression T-trees (trees with T leaves) - producing $K$ additive functions $f_k$, which combined, learn to map inputs to continuous values
\begin{equation}
    \hat y_i = \sum_{k=1}^K f_k(\boldsymbol{x}_i),
\end{equation}
subject to the regularisation objective
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L (\phi)= \sum_i l(\hat y_i, y_i)+\sum_k\Omega(f_k).
\end{equation}
Here, $l$ is a differentiable loss between the prediction $\hat y_i$ and target value $y_i$, and $\Omega(f)$ is a complexity penalising regulaisation term \cite{chen_xgboost_2016}. We assemble the ensemble of trees iteratively. At each iteration $t$, we add $f_t$ to the prediction at the previous iteration and minimise
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L^{(t)}&=\sum_{i=1}^nl(y_i, \hat y_i^{(t-1)}+f_t(\boldsymbol{x}_i))+\Omega(f_t)\\
    &\approx \sum_{i=1}^n \left[l(y_i, \hat y^{(t-1)})+g_i f_t (\boldsymbol{x}_i)+\frac{1}{2} h_if_t^2(\boldsymbol{x}_i)\right])+\Omega(f_t),
\end{align}
where $g_i=\partial_{\hat y^{(t-1)}} l(y_i, \hat y^{(t-1)})$ and $h_i=\partial^2_{\hat y^{(t-1)}}l(y_i, \hat y^{(t-1)})$ are first and second order gradients of the loss function \cite{friedman_additive_2000}. Minimising this loss over a leaf then gives the optimal tree weighting 
$w_j$ for that leaf. Many further optimisation techniques are used to improve the performance of XGBoost, the details of which can be found in \cite{chen_xgboost_2016}. A final prediction is then made by summing contributions from each tree, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:tree_ensemble}. Note that for $N$-classification tasks, $N$ trees - corresponding to positive and negative classification for each class - are built for each iteration, with softmax \cite{bridle_probabilistic_1990} over the summed outputs from these trees used to selected the best classification.

\subsection{Neural Networks}
Neutral networks \cite{schmidhuber_deep_2015,goodfellow_deep_2016,lussier_deep_2020} are machine learning algorithms built from layers of connected nodes (neurons) acted on by activation functions (§\ref{appendix:Activations}). The weights and biases associated with each neuron are then learnt by using backpropagation \cite{rumelhart_learning_1986} and gradient descent \cite{ruder_overview_2017} to minimise a loss function. This finds a mapping from inputs to outputs. With suitable model design, regularisation \cite{goodfellow_deep_2016,kukacka_regularization_2017} and training parameter (hyperparameter) selection, these mappings can be tuned to generalise well to unseen data. Throughout this work, networks are trained, validated and tested on independent folds of data as described in §\ref{sec:methods:data_split}, the Adam optimiser \cite{kingma_adam_2017} is used for gradient descent, and hyperparameter tuning is conducted on the validation split using the Weights and Biases tool \cite{biewald_experiment_2020}. \subsubsection{A Scale-Adjusting Convolutional Neural Network (SCNN)}
\label{sec:methods:scnn}

In this work, a SCNN similar to that presented by Zaki et al. \cite{zaki_explainable_2024} is implemented for feature extraction from 801 dimensional FTIR spectra. The model architecture is summarised in Figure \ref{fig:scnn_arch}. Notable features include the use of three scanning convolutional heads to extract information at different spectral scales, which is important for capturing the variable-width peaks in FTIR spectra. Furthermore, the use of $\tanh$ followed by ReLU activation functions reduces the effect of unphysically large convolutional outputs, which could otherwise produce exploding gradients \cite{song_fine-grained_nodate,li_learning_2020,zaki_explainable_2024}. Finally, the last layers in the network learn a three parameter logistic output \cite{yu_three-parameter_2021};
\begin{equation} %Px = c+(1-c)/(1+torch.exp(-a*(x-b)))
    \boldsymbol{\hat y} = c+ \frac{(1-c)}{1+\exp(-a(\boldsymbol{x}-b))},
\end{equation}
where $c$ is a guess parameter, $b$ denotes difficulty and $a$ denotes the discrimination. This allows the model to find a baseline offset for categorisation probability corresponding to the how it could sometimes be impossible to distinguish between classes using only FTIR data.

\subsection{Explainability}
\subsubsection{Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME)}
LIME \cite{ribeiro_why_2016} is a model-agnostic explainability technique which works by producing $N$ perturbations around an input vector, $\boldsymbol{x}$ and observing how perturbing different features alters classification probabilities. In this work, a variant of this is employed where each of the $d$ input features (wavelength intensities) are one-hot encoded \cite{} into a vector, $\boldsymbol{x'}$ of length 10, with the encoded value corresponding to the training-set decile in which that feature value lies. Perturbed samples, $\boldsymbol{z'}$, are then generated by considering each feature, either retaining their one-hot encoding with probability $1/2$, or uniformly allocating a new one-hot encoding, also with probability $1/2$. A numeric vector $\boldsymbol{z}$ is then generated by randomly sampling within the deciles associated with each value in $\boldsymbol{z'}$. We then aim to train a local linear model $g\in \mathcal G$ to approximate the behaviour of the original model we wish to explain, $f$, around $\boldsymbol{x}$. Here, $\mathcal G$ is a class of potentially interpretable models. The selected LIME explanation is then given by:
\begin{equation}
    \xi(x)=\underset{g\in \mathcal G}{\text{argmin}} \left[ \mathcal L(f,g,\pi_x)+\Omega(g)\right],
    \label{eq:explanation}
\end{equation}
where the locality-aware loss $\mathcal L(f,g,\pi_x)$ encodes the faithfulness of $g$ to $f$ in the locality $\pi_x$ and $\Omega(g)$ is an explanation-complexity penality term. This process is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:lime_illustration}. In this work we are considering classification tasks, so the locality-aware loss is calculated using a square loss between the predicted probability for positive classification as given by $f$ and $g$ (the LIME process is repeated for each class). This takes the form:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L(f,g,\pi_x)=\sum_{z, z'}\pi_x(z)\left(f(z)-g(z')\right)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\pi_x(z)=\exp(-D(x,z)^2/\sigma^2)$ encodes locality through an L2 \cite{} distance function $D$ and $\sigma$ is generally set as the median of all $D$ values. We then solve \ref{eq:explanation} using a ridge regressor over $N$ perturbed samples for each sample to be explained, giving \cite{}: 
\begin{equation}
    (\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{b}) = \underset{\boldsymbol{w}, \boldsymbol{b}}{\text{argmin}} \sum_{\boldsymbol{z}, \boldsymbol{z'}} \pi_x(\boldsymbol{z})\left[f(\boldsymbol{z})-\left(\boldsymbol{w}^T \boldsymbol{z'}+\boldsymbol{b}\right)\right]^2+\lambda |\boldsymbol{w}|^2_2,
\end{equation}
where the weights $\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{dx10}$ and biases $\boldsymbol{b}\in \mathbb{R}^d$ are those of the linear model $g$ for a single class in a single sample. Calculating the $l_2$ norm of the $10$ weights for each feature then produces relative importance scores for each feature within class $k$, $\boldsymbol s_k$. Finally, the total importance score for each feature is calculated by performing a probability-weighted sum over all classes \cite{}: 
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{s} = \sum_k p_k(\boldsymbol x) \boldsymbol s_k.
\end{equation}
An example of the LIME weightings this process produces is shown in Figure \ref{fig:lime_weights_example}.

\subsubsection{Context Representative Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (CRIME)}
\label{sec:methods:crime}
The CRIME method \cite{zaki_explainable_2024} aims to expand on the LIME method by finding contexts which have similar classification explanations. This is achieved by passing the LIME results through a variational eutoencoder (VAE) \cite{} to learn a low-dimensional latent space representation of the explanations. This latent space is then clustered using $K$-means clustering \cite{} to produce $K$ clusters, each of which can be investigated. In their original paper, Zaki et al. \cite{zaki_explainable_2024} used a VAE similar to that described in §\ref{sec:vae_zaki}, and were able to form six contexts from surface enhanced Raman (SER) urine spectra, of which three were found to be associated with serotonin. Here, I expand on this work by investigating the use of different VAE architectures as described in §\ref{sec:methods:vae}, and by using the clusters themselves perform explainable linear classification.

\subsubsection{Variational Autoencoders (VAE)}
\label{sec:vae}

Attempting to find clusters of the explanations produced by LIME can be viewed as either an unsupervised learning problem - clustering the LIME spectra with no additional information, or as a semi-supervised learning problem, where known training labels can be used to inform clustering. In this work, I implemented both approaches by noting that the semi-supervised approach reduces to the unsupervised approach when the supervision level is zero. This is implemented using a VAE, which attempts to learn a generative model with parameters $\theta$ such as to enable mapping from the data $\boldsymbol{x}$ to a latent representation $\boldsymbol{z}$ and back again - as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:VAE}. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Images/VAE_overview.png}
  \caption{An illustration of the mapping from input values to a latent space and back to prediction values within a VAE. From \cite{kingma_introduction_nodate}}
  \label{fig:VAE}
\end{figure}

\noindent
An unsupervised VAE must learn a joint distribution $p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})$ such as to maximise the marginal likelihood of the data:
\begin{align}
    p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}) &= \int_{\boldsymbol z} p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})d\boldsymbol{z} \\
    & = \int_{\boldsymbol z} p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z}) p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})d\boldsymbol{z} \label{eq:intractable}
\end{align}
Equation (\ref{eq:intractable}), however is normally intractable. To overcome this, we use Bayes' theorem to rewrite the marginal likelihood as:
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}) = \frac{p_\theta(\boldsymbol {z})p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x} | \boldsymbol{z})}{p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})}=\frac{p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})}{p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})}.
\end{equation}
Therefore, we can also approach the problem by attempting to approximate the posterior $p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})$ by an amortised variational distribution $q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})$ with parameters $\phi$. By then expanding the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence \cite{}: 
\begin{equation}
    D_\text{KL}(q|p)=\int q(\boldsymbol{z})\log\left(\frac{q(\boldsymbol{z})}{p(\boldsymbol{z})}\right)d\boldsymbol{z}\ge 0,
    \label{eq:kl_div}
\end{equation}
which measures the information entropy of $q_\phi$ relative to $p_\theta$, we can write the marginal likelihood as:
\begin{align}
    \log p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}) &= D_\text{KL}(q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})|p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x}))+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})}}[\log p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x})-\log q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})] \\
    \implies \log p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}) & \ge \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})}\left[\log p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}) - \log q_\phi(\boldsymbol {z}|\boldsymbol{x})\right]=\text{ELBO},
\end{align}
where the term on the RHS of the inequality is the evidence lower bound (ELBO) \cite{}. The ELBO gives a lower bound on the marginal likelihood, so maximising it also minimises the KL divergence. This maximisation, however is non-trivial because the $\phi$ derivative of the ELBO is intractable and non-estimable. To overcome this, we employ the reparameterisation trick \cite{}, where we deterministically re-express $\boldsymbol{z}$ as:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{z} = g(\phi, \boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ is a random variable generated through a Gaussian distribution with mean $\boldsymbol{0}$ and unit variance \cite{SGVB_gradient_variational_bayes}. Finally, we can estimate the gradient of the negative $-\text{ELBO}=\mathcal L(\boldsymbol{x})$ (to perform gradient descent) as:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L(\boldsymbol{x}) &= -\mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\left[\log p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z}, \boldsymbol{x})-\log q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})\right]\\
    \implies \nabla_{\theta, \phi}\mathcal L(\boldsymbol{x})&=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\nabla_{\theta,\phi}\left[\log p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z}, \boldsymbol{x})-\log q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})\right],
\end{align}
where $N$ is the number of samples in a batch. This formulation allows us to approximate the posterior by gradient descent. The ELBO can now be expanded to give: 
\begin{equation}
    \text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\left[\log p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})\right]-D_\text{KL}\left(q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})|p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z})\right).
\end{equation}
If we then assume the prior $p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})$, and that the distribution $q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})$ is a multivariate Gaussian with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and variance $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ where $\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}=f(\boldsymbol{x})$, we can rewrite the KL divergence \ref{eq:kl_div} as: 
\begin{equation}
    D_\text{KL}(q_\phi(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x})|p_\theta(\boldsymbol{z}))=-\frac{1}{2} \left(1+\log(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^2-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2\right).
\end{equation}
Furthermore, we use single-sample monte carlo estimation \cite{} to find the expectation of the log-likelihood term subject to the assumption that $p_\theta(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol{y}_\mu, 1)$, which reduces to the form:
\begin{equation}
    \log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}_\mu\right)^2=-\frac{1}{2}\text{MSE}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}_\mu).
\end{equation}
Hence, we can optimise such a VAE by minimising the negative ELBO:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L(\boldsymbol{x}) = -\frac{1}{2}\text{MSE}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}_\mu)+\beta \frac{1}{2}\left[1+\log(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)-\boldsymbol{\mu}^2-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2\right],
\end{equation}
which is adopted as the unsupervised VAE loss function throughout this work, where $\beta$ is a tunable parameter \cite{beta-vae}. When using a semi-supervised VAE approach, we follow the M2 approach described by Kingma et al. \cite{}, so modify this loss function to include a classification term by adding a cross-entropy loss \cite{} assessing the difference between predicted and true class probabilities. These predicted class probabilities are given by softmax over the output from a two-layer ($64\rightarrow128\rightarrow4$ node) fully-connected layer at the end of the VAE encoder, prior to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ projection. These implementations of these ELBOs and the reparameterisation trick can be found in \ref{sec:code:ELBOs} and \ref{sec:code:reparam} respectively. The VAE architectures used in this work are summarised in Table \ref{tab:VAE_architectures}, and their full implementations can be found in \ref{appendix:code:VAEs}. As each VAE is already implicitly using a model trained on the training set for each split, the VAE itself is trained on the LIME explanations of the validation set.

\subsubsection{CRIME with Linear Classification}
\label{sec:crime_linear}
Once the CRIME method has been used to cluster the LIME explanations into contexts, the mean FTIR spectrum associated with the LIME explanations in each context can be found. This work then uses these mean spectra, along with the mean LIME weights for each context to produce an explainable linear classification model. This is achieved by first classifying each context according to the most common classification (normal, hyperplastic, adenoma, or cancer) of the spectra which are placed into the context when training the VAE on the validation set. We then compute a LIME-importance-weighted cosine similarity between each mean cluster spectrum and a test spectrum. The method used to calculate this similarity was initially developed by Zaki et al. to calculate the similarity of their clustered spectra to known indicator spectra \cite{zaki_explainable_2024}. For each clustered spectrum, $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, with weights $\boldsymbol{w}$, we scale both to a range $[-1,1]$ according to the minimum and maximum values within each vector, giving scaled vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}$. We then do the same for a test spectrum $\boldsymbol{t}\rightarrow \hat{\boldsymbol{t}}$. The cosine similarity is then calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}^T (\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \cdot \hat{\boldsymbol{w}})}{|\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}|^2 |\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \cdot \hat{\boldsymbol{w}}|^2}.
\end{equation}
Each test set spectrum is then classified according to the context with which is achieves the highest similarity score. The respective context classification is then used to map the test spectra to a physical classification (normal, hyperplastic, adenoma or cancer). Due to the low number of independent samples, it is not possible to further divide the dataset to find a reliable validation set for this method, so sensible VAE parameters are selected based on their performance classifying the validation set before performing a final run to evaluate performance on the test set.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The dataset used in this work is highly imbalanced, so a very high accuracy can be achieved by simply predicting the most common class. To avoid this, I calculated performance metrics relative to each "true" classification, $k$, using the following confusion matrix $(\mathrm{CM})$ (see Figure \ref{fig:confusion_matrix}) conventions:
\begin{align}
\text{TP}_k &= \mathrm{CM}_{kk}, &
\text{FN}_k &= \sum_j \mathrm{CM}_{kj} - \text{TP}_k, \\
\text{FP}_k &= \sum_i \mathrm{CM}_{ik} - \text{TP}_k, &
\text{TN}_k &= \sum_{i,j} \mathrm{CM}_{ij} - \bigl(\text{TP}_k+\text{FN}_k+\text{FP}_k\bigr).
\end{align}

\noindent From these counts we then define:
\begin{align}
\text{Sensitivity}_k &= \frac{\text{TP}_k}{\text{TP}_k+\text{FN}_k}, &
\text{Specificity}_k &= \frac{\text{TN}_k}{\text{TN}_k+\text{FP}_k}, \\[4pt]
\text{Accuracy}_k   &= \frac{\text{TP}_k+\text{TN}_k}
                            {\text{TP}_k+\text{TN}_k+\text{FP}_k+\text{FN}_k}, &
\text{FPR}_k        &= \frac{\text{FP}_k}{\text{FP}_k+\text{TN}_k}.
\end{align}

\noindent Finally, to produce a metric which fairly measures performance despite dataset imbalance, we define the weighted overall accuracy as: 
\begin{align}
\text{Weighted Overall Accuracy} &= \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k \cdot \text{Accuracy}_k}{\sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k}, \\ \text{where} \quad w_k &= \frac{\text{True number of samples in class } k}{\text{Total number of samples}}.
\label{eq:overall_accuracy}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Binary Classification Methodology}
\label{sec:methods:binary}
Due to the high computational cost in training the SCNN networks, it was decided that binary classification performance results throughout this work would be calculated using four-class classification predictions. For this, we categorise cancer, hyperplastic and adenomatous predictions as non-cancerous and cancerous predictions are unchanged.
